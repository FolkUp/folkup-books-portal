# What This Book Is About: «Agile Sapiens»

In 2001, seventeen people gathered at a ski resort in the mountains of Utah and fit onto a single page everything they thought about proper work: in short steps, with live feedback, revising the plan as they went, because life is smarter than the plan. That one page has since fed an entire industry — certifications, coaches, trainings, multi-volume codices on how to be flexible. The market runs to tens of billions of dollars and grows every year.

The book opens with a suspicion: those seventeen invented nothing. They remembered.

And it proves the point in a strange way. In April 1846, Edgar Allan Poe published an essay in which he took apart his own «Raven» like an engineer — on effect, tone, length, climax — and declared that great literature is not inspiration but calculation. This book performs the same operation in reverse: it decomposes modern management systems into literary archetypes. The method has a name — literary analysis of business.

Why the roundabout route? For a single argument, on which everything else rests.

When a consulting firm publishes a study on corporate transformation, it is selling corporate transformation services. When a certifying body extols the effectiveness of agile methods, it is selling certificates. But when Jules Verne describes the circumnavigation of Phileas Fogg, he is selling an adventure novel to a newspaper. There is a commercial interest — but it has nothing to do with management. Verne does not know he is describing an iterative method; he is just telling a good story. That is exactly why the description comes out clean. Literature works as a control group: if a principle surfaces even where no one meant to sell it, then it is a principle and not packaging.

From here comes the central term of the book — the paradox of the obvious: the more evident a principle, the more expensive its formalization. A street is closed — you take the detour without convening a change management committee. In a corporation, the same capacity demands courses, certified specialists, and a multi-year transformation program. Fogg circled the globe with one servant and a newspaper timetable.

From here on, the dissections, and in each of them literature makes the diagnosis before management does.

Verne, together with Dickens and the Japanese rakugo masters, invents release-by-installment with reader feedback — in London, Paris, and Tokyo, independently of one another, which is exactly what gives the pattern away. Mary Shelley describes a creator who recoils from his result and flees the laboratory — long before the largest failures of digital transformation. Sherlock Holmes gives the method of observation: he knows there are seventeen steps because he did not merely see them but counted them. In «The Lottery in Babylon», Borges shows how measurement, conceived as a tool, becomes the only reality in which the system is still capable of describing itself — long before economists formalize the same thing as a law.

Then the book turns to teams. The engineer Cyrus Smith, with a handful of companions, builds a civilization on an uninhabited island without a single sign-off; Captain Nemo stands beside him as the counterexample — a genius in a submarine who left no successors. Mina Harker, a schoolmistress, in one night types up the diaries, letters, and wax cylinders of others into a single document — and only from that moment does the hunt for Dracula become possible. Doctor Jekyll turns out to be a precise portrait of the gap between what an organization declares about itself and what it does on Friday evening at six; the underground millionaire Koreiko, incidentally, fits beside him rather well.

And then the book looks forward. Wells — on how technology remakes the structure of labor, and beside him Platonov with «The Foundation Pit». The Strugatskys — on the team you cannot hire, because meaning is what assembles it: at NIICHAVO, Monday begins on Saturday, and not by the labor code. And Camus — on the people who did not choose to be heroes, who simply did not leave the plague-locked city and went on doing their work.

Between the dissections come the interludes, where analysis gives way to open ridicule: Carroll's Alice ends up at a consultancy where teams and meetings are past counting and there is not a single product, and the cat with the sandwich explains to her why one task can hang in every column of the board at once.

It ends with what one does not usually expect from such a book. Not conclusions, not recipes — questions. And the author warns straight off: there will be no table of «if the situation is such, ask this question», because the whole book is written against such tables. The question you reach for first will say more about your team than the situation itself.

The reservations worth hearing in advance. The book does not deny agile methods: stand-ups, retrospectives, and short cycles are ordinary working practices, invented long before anyone gave them names. It objects to the transformation of practice into institution, of ritual into fetish, of a language into an industry. It does not propose «being like Holmes», either: literature offers not role models but mirrors. And it certainly does not pine for the nineteenth century — the century of factory children and coal shafts; the books in question were simply written against the management that surrounded them, and are therefore not out of date.

And it ends on one line, which I might as well quote whole: the book is over, the team is not.

— Comandante FolkUp
