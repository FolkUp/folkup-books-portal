# What This Book Is About: «City of the Sun»

Four hundred years ago, the monk Campanella, sitting in an Inquisition prison, invented an ideal city — the City of the Sun. Everything there was arranged reasonably: seven concentric walls painted with all the world's knowledge, four-hour workdays, common property. And everything was prescribed: who conceives a child with whom is decided by an astrologer. One thing this city did not provide for: the desire to leave it. Ideal cities in general have a hard time with that question.

The book walks through the history of such cities: imagined, drafted, and sometimes built. From the heavenly Jerusalem of the Book of Revelation, through the utopians and the dreams of Vera Pavlovna, to the twentieth century, where the dream was finally let into concrete: Bauhaus, the Constructivists, house-communes, Brasília. And then the route turns where the history of utopias does not usually go — to those who decided not to build a city at all but to take it inside: a single pill instead of a century of history. The chemical utopia, from Herodotus's Scythians to Timothy Leary, turns out to be built on the same blueprint as the stone one, and fails in the same way.

One pair of concepts runs through the whole book, and it works as a watershed: image or project. An image prescribes direction but not the step; John's Jerusalem, which in principle cannot be built, or Angkor, which stood for eight centuries and outlived a change of religion. A project prescribes the step: Campanella, Le Corbusier, the master plan. The whole history of utopias, in essence, is about what happens when the project defeats the image: cities are built for a god, for a sovereign, for a state, for the «new man». For the resident, rarest of all; in the great projects the resident passes through as building material.

For this reason the book's litmus test is simple: is it comfortable here for an ordinary human? The one with grocery bags, with a child, who wants to walk to the bakery, sit in the courtyard, and have no draft in winter. The book has its own concept for this: residents as infrastructure. A courtyard where the neighbors are known by face, the bookshop on the corner where they remember what you took last time. This infrastructure appears nowhere on the models; it becomes visible, like an archipelago at low tide, most often at the moment of its loss.

The author tests this optic on himself: the book has a personal line about three cities in which he lived — Saint Petersburg, Setúbal, Berlin — and about what each taught. And the horizon of this curve the book finds in the most unexpected place: in a drawn city, where they make gondolas by hand, brew coffee slowly, and greet the neighbors. From this comes the closing formula: the City of the Sun is not built. The City of the Sun is practiced, and it spreads not by scaling, but by contagion — people see how it works for others and try it themselves.

This is the third book of the trilogy about how to live wisely and freely: method, access, and now environment. Because you can work right and get to the bottom of everything, but you still have to live in a city. Better if it's one that suits you, not a great one.
